\section{Conclusion}

This thesis proposes a survey experiment to examine how individuals in Germany form beliefs about the labor-market and social integration of Ukrainian and Syrian migrants. By independently varying migrants' country of origin, reason for migration, human capital, German-language proficiency, and gender, the factorial vignette design makes it possible to identify how these characteristics affect beliefs about employment, income, discrimination, and social integration. In particular, the design allows the role of country of origin to be examined while holding other migrant characteristics constant and tests whether differences between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants depend on refugee status and human capital.

Beyond these experimentally varied characteristics, the thesis considers how respondents' beliefs are related to the information and experiences available to them. Measures of personal contact, the quality of previous experiences, media exposure, and memorable personal and media information provide a way to examine whether differences in respondents' information environments are associated with their evaluations of migrant integration. The design therefore brings together two complementary perspectives: the characteristics of migrants that shape how they are perceived and the experiences and information through which respondents may form and update their beliefs. Additional measures of beliefs about migrant communities and integration over time provide further context for understanding these patterns.

The proposed study contributes to the literature by focusing on two migrant groups that entered Germany in different migration contexts and by comparing them within a common experimental framework. Rather than observing differences in attitudes toward Ukrainian and Syrian migrants without accounting for their other characteristics, the factorial design separates country of origin from characteristics such as refugee status, education, and language proficiency. At the same time, distinguishing the causal effects of experimentally varied profile characteristics from the associational evidence on respondents' experiences and information exposure provides a clear framework for interpreting the resulting evidence.

More broadly, understanding beliefs about migrant integration is important because public perceptions need not correspond directly to migrants' actual opportunities or outcomes. Examining which migrant characteristics are associated with more favorable or unfavorable beliefs, and how these beliefs relate to respondents' experiences and information environments, can provide evidence on how perceptions of different migrant groups are formed. The proposed design therefore offers a framework for studying not only whether Ukrainian and Syrian migrants are perceived differently in Germany, but also which characteristics and sources of information may be associated with these differences.
