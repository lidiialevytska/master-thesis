\section{Experimental Design}

This thesis proposes a survey experiment to examine how Germans form beliefs about the labor-market and social 
integration outcomes of Ukrainian and Syrian migrants. The proposed study consists of a factorial vignette experiment, 
a set of outcome measures, and a respondent questionnaire. The following sections describe each part of 
the proposed experimental design and the survey procedure.

\subsection{Target Population and Proposed Sample}

The target population consists of German citizens aged 18 or older who currently reside in Germany. Restricting 
eligibility to German citizens provides a clear operational definition of the German population whose beliefs about 
the integration of Ukrainian and Syrian migrants are the focus of this study. Similar citizenship-based sampling 
criteria have been used in previous studies examining attitudes toward refugees and migration in Germany 
\citep[e.g.,][]{hayo2023between}. Importantly, German citizenship is not used as a proxy for being native-born or 
having no migration background. Naturalized German citizens are therefore included in the target population, while 
respondents’ migration background is collected separately as a demographic characteristic in the respondent questionnaire.
At the beginning of the survey, respondents would therefore be screened with respect to age, country of residence, 
and German citizenship. Respondents who do not satisfy these eligibility criteria would not proceed to the experimental 
part of the survey.

I propose using Qualtrics to program the survey, and the online panel provider Bilendi for the recruitment of 
participants. Bilendi is suitable for the present study because it provides access to a large and diverse pool of 
respondents in Germany and allows the use of demographic quotas to approximate the composition of the target 
population. The proposed sample would use quotas for age, gender, education, and region. Respondents would receive 
compensation for their participation according to the standard compensation provided by the panel, taking into account 
the expected duration of the survey.

The final sample size would be determined based on a power analysis of the proposed experimental design. The number 
of vignette evaluations per respondent, which also affects the required sample size, is discussed in Section 3.2.

\subsection{Factorial Vignette Design}

The proposed study employs a factorial vignette experiment in which respondents evaluate hypothetical migrants whose 
characteristics are independently varied across profiles. This approach is particularly suitable for the present 
research question because migrants differ simultaneously along several characteristics. By independently varying these 
characteristics, the design makes it possible to distinguish their effects on respondents’ beliefs while holding the remaining 
characteristics constant. The proposed design builds on previous conjoint and factorial vignette experiments examining 
how migrant characteristics shape evaluations \citep{hainmueller2015hidden, bansak2016economic, czymara2016deutschland}.

The proposed vignettes describe migrants who have come to Germany and are currently looking for stable employment. 
Each profile varies independently along five binary dimensions: gender, country of origin, reason for migration, 
human capital, and German-language proficiency. 
These vignette attributes are selected to capture migrant characteristics that are central to the research questions 
and that previous research has shown to influence perceptions of migrants. Country of origin is varied between 
(1) Ukrainian and (2) Syrian migrants and represents the central comparison of the study. Reason for migration 
distinguishes between the two types of migrants: (1) refugees who fled the wars in Ukraine or Syria and (2) economic 
migrants who came to Germany in search of better employment opportunities. Educational attainment captures 
differences in migrants' human capital, which previous vignette and conjoint studies have shown to influence 
evaluations of immigrants \citep{hainmueller2010attitudes, hainmueller2015hidden, czymara2016deutschland}. 
The design distinguishes between (1) migrants without a formal vocational qualification and (2) migrants with a 
university degree. German-language proficiency is varied between (1) limited and (2) good German skills. 
Language proficiency may be relevant both for social integration and for labor-market outcomes, since knowledge of the 
host-country language represents an important form of human capital \citep{czymara2016deutschland}. 
Finally, gender distinguishes between (1) male and (2) female migrants. Gender is included because previous 
experimental research suggests that migrant gender can affect how immigrants and refugees are perceived, including in 
the German context \citep{czymara2017refugees, gereke2020gendered}. Randomizing gender therefore allows the effects 
of the other vignette characteristics to be estimated across both male and female migrant profiles.

As discussed previously, existing research shows that religion and perceived cultural distance can influence attitudes 
toward immigrants \citep[e.g.,][]{czymara2016deutschland}. However, in this study, religious affiliation is 
deliberately left unspecified. The main interest of this thesis is in the overall beliefs that respondents associate 
with Ukrainian and Syrian migrants. Explicitly including religion as an additional vignette attribute would not only 
make the design more complex, but could also change the beliefs that respondents would otherwise associate with the 
migrant's country of origin. For this reason, religion is not varied separately in the experiment. The estimated 
country-of-origin effect should therefore be interpreted as an overall effect of Ukrainian versus Syrian origin, 
which may also reflect perceived cultural or religious differences between the two groups.

The five binary attributes generate a full factorial design of \(2^5=32\) possible migrant profiles.
The vignettes would be worded as follows:\bigskip

A [male/female] from [Ukraine/Syria] came to Germany. [He/She] came to Germany as [a refugee after fleeing 
the war in his/her country / an economic migrant in search of better employment and economic opportunities in Germany]. 
[He/She] [has no formal vocational qualification / has completed a university degree], speaks German 
[only to a limited extent / well], and is currently looking for stable employment.\bigskip

Since evaluating 
all possible profiles would place a substantial burden on respondents, I propose that each respondent evaluate a 
randomly selected subset of six vignettes. This provides several profile evaluations per respondent while keeping 
the survey manageable, particularly because each vignette is followed by several belief questions about migrant outcomes. 
The choice therefore reflects a trade-off between obtaining sufficient statistical information and limiting 
cognitive burden and respondent fatigue, which are important considerations in factorial survey designs 
\citep{sauer2011application}. The exact number of vignettes could be further assessed through pilot testing.

Prior to implementation, the survey would be pilot tested with approximately 50 respondents from the target population. 
The pilot would be used to assess survey completion time, attrition, potential respondent fatigue, and the performance 
of the attention and data-quality checks. It would also be used to ensure that the vignette wording, questions, and 
response scales are understandable and that the Qualtrics randomization functions correctly. In particular, dropout 
patterns and response times across the survey would be examined to assess whether six vignette evaluations can reasonably 
be completed without imposing excessive burden. Based on the pilot results and respondent feedback, the exact number of 
vignettes and, where necessary, the wording or survey structure could be adjusted before the main study. The order in 
which the vignette profiles are presented to each respondent would also be randomized.

\subsection{Outcome Variables}
The five outcome questions would be answered right after each vignette. They are designed to capture respondents' 
beliefs about the labor-market, discrimination, and social-integration outcomes of the migrant described in each profile.

The outcomes cover three aspects: beliefs about labor-market integration outcomes (expected employment and expected income), 
beliefs about labor-market discrimination (expected hiring and workplace treatment), and beliefs about social 
integration. The exact wording of all outcome questions and response scales is provided in Appendix [X].

The first two outcome questions capture beliefs about the migrant's labor-market integration. 
As discussed in Section 2.2, labor-market integration is reflected in several dimensions, including employment, 
earnings, and occupational outcomes \citep{brell2020labor, fasani2024refugees}. Therefore, to capture the clearest
and the most interpretable dimensions, the proposed experiment measures respondents' beliefs about labor-market 
integration based on expected employment and expected income.
First, respondents state the probability that the migrant will find regular paid employment subject to social security 
contributions within the next two years. The type of employment in this question is formulated in a way to exclude 
informal, marginal, or unstable jobs. The timeframe of two years is chosen because it is long enough for job search, 
language learning, and recognition of qualifications of the migrants in Germany, but still short enough for respondents 
to imagine. Responses would be recorded on a scale from 0 to 100 percent. This probability scale provides a direct 
quantitative measure of respondents' beliefs about employment prospects. 
Second, respondents indicate the migrant's expected monthly net income five years from now by selecting one of seven 
income categories.

Two additional measures capture respondents' beliefs about discrimination in the German labor market. The first concerns 
hiring discrimination and asks respondents how likely is it that the migrant would be treated less favorably in the 
hiring process than an otherwise comparable German applicant. The second concerns discrimination after entering 
employment and measures the expected likelihood of unequal treatment in the workplace, for example in areas such as 
wages, promotion opportunities, or interactions with colleagues. Both outcomes are measured on seven-point scales, 
with higher values indicating greater agreement and therefore higher expected discrimination.

The final outcome captures beliefs about social integration. Respondents assess how likely the migrant is to 
successfully integrate socially in Germany within the next five years. To give respondents a common understanding of 
social integration, the question defines it as the migrants building regular social contacts with German citizens within
the next five years. Responses are recorded on a seven-point scale, with higher values indicating more favorable 
beliefs about social integration.

Combined, these five measures capture different dimensions of respondents' beliefs about migrant integration rather 
than a single overall evaluation. The labor-market variables provide the main outcomes for H1a, H1b, H2, and H3a, 
while the discrimination measures provide the outcomes for H3b and the social-integration measure provides an additional 
outcome for H1b and H3a. All five outcome measures are used to examine H3c. In the empirical 
analysis, the outcome measures are analyzed separately rather than being combined into a single integration index.

\subsection{Respondent Questionnaire}

After answering all outcome questions for the vignette profiles, respondents would complete an additional questionnaire 
measuring personal contact experiences, media exposure, general beliefs, and their background characteristics. 
These questions are specifically asked after the vignette experiment to avoid influencing respondents' answers 
to the vignettes. For questions that are asked separately for Ukrainian and Syrian migrants, the order of the two 
groups would be randomized where feasible.

\subsubsection{Personal Contact and Experience.}

\noindent\textbf{Contact frequency.} Respondents are asked separately about the frequency of workplace and non-work 
contact with Ukrainian and Syrian migrants during the past 12 months. Non-work contact refers to interactions outside 
of a workplace setting, for example as friends, neighbours, acquaintances, through clubs, or in other social settings. 
Both questions use the same seven-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (several times per day).
For more details on the exact wording of the questions and response scales, see Appendix [X].
For each migrant group, the two measures are combined into an overall contact exposure measure \(C_{ig}\):

$$ C_{ig}=\frac{C^{\mathrm{Work}}_{ig}+C^{\mathrm{Non-Work}}_{ig}}{2}, $$

where \(C^{\mathrm{Work}}_{ig}\) and \(C^{\mathrm{Non-Work}}_{ig}\) denote workplace and non-work contact, respectively, for 
respondent \(i\) and migrant group \(g\), where \(g \in \{\text{Ukrainian, Syrian}\}\). Higher values indicate 
more frequent overall contact with the respective group. If a person is retired, unemployed, a student, or not currently 
working, they would answer that workplace contact is not applicable for them and it would be marked as missing instead 
of reporting it as never having contact with the migrant group in the workplace. 

This contact measure can be used in the specifications for H4–H6, because these hypotheses concern respondents' 
overall contact experiences with the migrant group. Additionally, the two contact types can be analyzed separately 
to examine whether workplace and non-work contact are differently associated with respondents' beliefs because contact 
in the workplace is not necessarily comparable to social contact with friends or neighbours.\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Overall contact quality.} If respondents report having had contact with Ukrainian or Syrian migrants, 
they are asked to evaluate the overall quality of their contact experiences with each group. The question is answered
on a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (very negative) to 7 (very positive), with 4 representing a neutral 
experience that is neither positive nor negative. This measure is denoted by \(Q_{ig}\) and is used to examine H4a, 
conditional on respondents' overall contact exposure \(C_{ig}\).

To examine whether negative experiences are more strongly associated with respondents' beliefs than positive experiences, 
as proposed in H4b, the overall experience measure is additionally separated into positive and negative components:

$$ Positive_{ig}=\max(Q_{ig}-4,0), $$ $$ Negative_{ig}=\max(4-Q_{ig},0). $$

where \(Positive_{ig}\) captures the positivity of respondents' overall contact experiences and 
\(Negative_{ig}\) captures the negativity of respondents' overall contact experiences. Both of them are designed to range 
from 0 to 3 with higher values indicating more positive or negative experiences, respectively. Both take the value zero
from a neutral response of \(Q_{ig}=4\). These separate measures make it possible to examine whether negative and 
positive experiences are differently associated with respondents' beliefs.\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Most memorable experience.} If respondents report having had contact with Ukrainian or Syrian migrants,
they are also asked to state whether a particular experience immediately comes to mind when thinking about their 
personal experiences. They would only respond with ``Yes'' or ``No''.\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Valence of most memorable experience.} If their answer is ``Yes'', they are asked to evaluate this 
most memorable experience, denoted by \(E_{ig}\), on a seven-point scale. The valence of the 
most memorable experience is denoted by \(V_{ig}^{\mathrm{E}}\) and is used to examine H6a 
while controlling for overall contact frequency and quality. For respondents who report having had contact with a 
migrant group but do not have a particular experience that comes to mind, \(V^{E}_{ig}\) is coded as missing.

\subsubsection{Media Exposure and Information.}

\noindent\textbf{Media exposure.} Respondents are asked how often they come across information about Ukrainian and 
Syrian migrants living in Germany through media channels such as television, newspapers, online news, social media, 
radio, podcasts, etc. The question is asked separately for each migrant group using a seven-point scale ranging from 
1 (never) to 7 (several times per day). The resulting measure of media-exposure frequency is denoted by \(M_{ig}\), 
with higher values indicating more frequent exposure to media information.\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Overall media tone.} Respondents are then asked to evaluate the tone (valence) of the media information they 
have encountered about each migrant group using a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (very negative) to 7 (very positive), 
with 4 representing neutral or balanced information. The resulting measure, \(T_{ig}\), captures the perceived tone 
of the media information. This measure is used to examine H5 while controlling for the frequency of media exposure 
\(M_{ig}\).\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Most memorable media information.} If respondents have had media exposure, similar to the most memorable 
personal experience, respondents are asked whether a particular piece of media information immediately comes to mind. 
They would respond with either ``Yes'' or ``No''. The most memorable piece of media information is denoted 
by \(I_{ig}\) which stands for information.\bigskip 

\noindent\textbf{Valence of most memorable media information.} 
Respondents who recall a particular piece of media information, \(I_{ig}\), are asked to evaluate it on a seven-point 
scale. The valence of the most memorable media information is denoted by \(V^{\mathrm{I}}_{ig}\), with higher values 
indicating more positive recalled information. This measure is used to examine H6b while controlling for media-exposure 
frequency and overall media tone.

The measures of memorable personal experiences and media information differ from controlled experimental settings in 
which researchers manipulate the experiences participants encounter \citep{schwerter2020determinants} or observe the 
information participants receive and can subsequently assess recall against this information \citep{graeber2024stories}. 
In the present thesis, both personal experiences and media information exposure occur naturally as positive or negative experiences 
in the lives of the respondents and are not observed by the researcher. The measures therefore capture respondents' 
current subjective evaluations of the information that is most salient in memory rather than its objective valence or recall accuracy.
This is relevant for the present thesis because the focus is on how respondents' own perceptions of certain experiences 
and information are associated with their beliefs.

\subsubsection{General Beliefs About Integration.}
The questionnaire also includes some questions on more general group-level beliefs about the integration of Ukrainian and Syrian 
migrants in Germany, capturing respondents' broader perceptions of the two migrant groups. These measures are not used 
as primary outcomes for H1–H6. Instead, they can be used in descriptive and exploratory analyses that may help interpret 
mechanism behind why respondents' beliefs differ between Ukrainians and Syrians.\bigskip 

\noindent\textbf{Integration over time.} Integration itself can also develop with time spent in the host country and 
can also differ across dimensions such as labor-market and social integration.
Previous research shows that migrants' labor-market integration tends to improve with duration of residence, including 
through higher employment and earnings, while initial labor-market disadvantages can narrow over time 
\citep{brucker2020funf,fasani2022struggle}. There is also supporting evidence for social integration improving over time. 
Longer residence can be associated with greater interaction with the host-country population and stronger social 
integration \citep{niehues2021spracherwerb, hannafi2023social}. Such developments may also shape the beliefs of 
the general German population about whether migrants become better integrated the longer they live in Germany. 

Therefore, the questionnaire is designed to measure these two beliefs. Respondents are asked whether they agree 
with the statements that (1) migrants become better integrated into the German labor market the longer they live in Germany,
and that (2) they become more socially integrated the longer they live in Germany. Responses are 
evaluated on a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The resulting measures are 
denoted by \(TimeLM_i\) and \(TimeSoc_i\), capturing general beliefs that labor-market and social integration, respectively, 
improve with duration of residence.\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Establishment of Co-Ethnic Communities.} The potential role of co-ethnic networks may depend 
on how established migrant communities are perceived to be. Respondents are therefore asked whether they agree
that the Ukrainian and Syrian communities in Germany are well established. Responses are recorded on a seven-point scale. 
The resulting measure is denoted by \(Comm_{ig}\), with higher values indicating a stronger belief that the respective 
migrant community is well established in Germany. \bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Community Networks: Labor-Market Facilitation.} Respondents may hold significant beliefs about the 
different integration outcomes associated with community networks. The first mechanism is that co-ethnic 
communities may facilitate migrants' labor-market integration by providing access to information and personal 
networks that can help them find employment. Evidence from Germany shows that immigrants located in areas 
with larger co-ethnic networks are more likely to find employment shortly after arrival and are more likely to find 
their first job through personal contacts \citep{battisti2022dynamic}. Respondents are therefore asked whether they agree 
that co-ethnic communities in Germany help newly arrived migrants find employment using a seven-point scale. 
The measure is denoted by \(FacilEmp_{ig}\). \bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Community Networks: Reduced Interaction with Germans.} The second mechanism is that co-ethnic 
communities may also affect social integration by reducing migrants' interaction with the host-country population. 
Evidence from Germany suggests that greater access to co-ethnic communities can reduce social interaction with natives 
and may also negatively affect the language ability of migrants \citep{danzer2013ethnic, danzer2016ethnic}. 
Respondents are therefore asked whether they agree with the statement that being able to rely on established 
Ukrainian and Syrian communities reduces the need for newly arrived migrants to interact regularly with Germans. 
Responses are recorded on a seven-point scale. The resulting measure is denoted by \(RedInt_{ig}\).

\subsubsection{Respondent Controls.}
Lastly, the questionnaire collects respondent controls which are standard demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. These include 
respondents' age, gender, education, employment status, household income, region of residence in Germany, migration 
background, religious affiliation, and political orientation. These variables are used to describe 
the sample and as control variables. The exact wording, response categories, and coding of these variables are provided 
in Appendix [X].

\subsection{Data Quality and Processing.}

Insufficient attention and careless responding can reduce the quality of data collected through self-administered online 
surveys, making procedures for identifying potentially low-quality responses important \citep{berinsky2014separating, curran2016methods}. 
The proposed survey would therefore include a simple instructed-response attention check and would record survey 
completion times. Available data-quality controls provided by Qualtrics and the Bilendi panel would additionally be used, 
where applicable, to identify duplicate or automated responses.

Respondents would be flagged for potential low-quality responding based on failure of the attention check or implausibly 
short completion times. The precise criteria for identifying such responses would be specified following pilot testing 
and prior to the main analysis. Rather than automatically excluding respondents based on a single indicator, the 
different indicators of response quality would be considered together. Robustness analyses could additionally examine 
whether the results are sensitive to the inclusion of respondents flagged for potential low-quality responding.

Data processing would distinguish between low-quality responses and legitimate missing or not-applicable responses. 
Displayed survey questions would generally require a response before respondents could proceed. Where appropriate, 
particularly for sensitive respondent characteristics, an explicit “prefer not to say” option would be provided. 
Missing values may therefore arise from such explicit non-response options or from conditional questions that are not 
displayed based on respondents’ preceding answers. These values would not automatically result in respondent-level 
exclusion but would be treated according to the respective variable.
Further details on the attention check, exclusion criteria, treatment and coding of missing responses, 
and construction of derived variables would be provided in Appendix [X].
