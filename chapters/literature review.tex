\section{Literature Review and Conceptual Framework}

\subsection{Immigration Attitudes and Migrant Characteristics}

A large literature examines how host-country populations evaluate migrants and which characteristics shape 
these evaluations. One central distinction in this literature is based on whether attitudes toward migrants 
primarily reflect individuals' own economic interests and labor-market considerations or characteristics of the migrants.

Earlier economic studies provide some evidence that labor-market considerations are associated with 
immigration preferences \citep[e.g.,][]{scheve2001labor, mayda2006against}. Beyond economic self-interest, immigration 
attitudes have also been linked to broader fiscal, cultural, and group-related concerns \citep{burns2000economic, dustmann2007racial, sides2007european}. 
Reviewing this literature, \citet{hainmueller2014public} conclude that evidence for explanations based on 
individual economic self-interest is generally limited.

Experimental evidence further challenges a simple labor-market competition explanation and rather points to the 
importance of migrants' own characteristics. \citet{hainmueller2010attitudes} find that both high- and low-skilled 
natives prefer high-skilled over low-skilled immigrants. If immigration attitudes were primarily driven by 
competition with similarly skilled workers, preferences would be expected to differ according to respondents' 
own skill levels. Instead, the common preference for high-skilled immigration suggests that respondents place 
considerable weight on migrants' human capital and potential economic contribution. This pattern is also found in 
multidimensional experiments. Higher education, occupational status, employability, and language proficiency are 
generally associated with more favorable evaluations of migrants \citep{hainmueller2015hidden, bansak2016economic,czymara2016deutschland}. 
More recent evidence from Germany similarly shows greater willingness to accept 
skilled economic migrants with recognized professional qualifications, good language skills, and a definite job offer 
\citep{wolff2025skilled}. Taken together, these studies show that characteristics associated with migrants' expected 
labor-market integration play an important role in how they are evaluated.

Country of origin, cultural and religious 
background, and migration status also influence attitudes when other characteristics are held constant. 
\citet{hainmueller2015hidden} find differences in evaluations across immigrants' countries of origin alongside 
effects of education, occupation, and English proficiency. In the European context, \citet{bansak2016economic} show that 
preferences over asylum seekers depend jointly on country 
of origin, religion, employability, and vulnerability. Similar patterns emerge in 
Germany. \citet{czymara2016deutschland} find that acceptance varies with migrants' country of origin and reason for migration
 as well as their education and German-language proficiency. These studies show that evaluations depend not only 
on migrants’ individual characteristics, but also on broader group-related characteristics and migration categories.

Although much of this literature focuses on attitudes and preferences toward migrants rather than beliefs about their 
expected outcomes, it shows that evaluations vary systematically with migrants’ observable characteristics. 
In line with this literature, higher human capital and stronger language proficiency may similarly serve as signals when 
respondents form expectations about migrants’ economic and social integration. This leads to the first hypotheses: \bigskip

H1a: Holding other migrant characteristics constant, respondents expect migrants with higher human capital to achieve 
more favorable labor-market integration outcomes than migrants with lower human capital. \bigskip

H1b: Holding other migrant characteristics constant, respondents expect migrants with higher German-language 
proficiency to achieve more favorable labor-market and social-integration outcomes than migrants with lower 
German-language proficiency.

\subsection{Beliefs and Expectations about Migrant Outcomes}

A growing literature shows that individuals hold systematic beliefs about immigrants and their economic and 
social characteristics. Much of this research examines perceptions of immigrant populations as a whole, including 
their size, composition, education, and employment \citep{sides2007european, alesina2023immigration}. 
Respondents tend to overestimate the share of immigrants living in their country, and perceive immigrants 
as less educated, more unemployed, and more dependent on government transfers than they actually are. 
However, these perceptions may depend on which types of migrants respondents have in mind. \citet{blinder2015imagined} 
shows that public understandings of the term “immigrant” do not necessarily correspond to the full migrant 
population, with some migrant categories being considerably more salient than others. This suggests that treating 
migrants as a single category may obscure differences in how distinct types of migrants are perceived. 

One particularly relevant distinction concerns migrants’ reasons for migration. Evaluations vary between refugees and 
migrants arriving for economic reasons \citep{czymara2016deutschland}, while refugees also tend to experience less 
favorable actual labor-market outcomes than economic migrants, including lower employment, occupational, and earnings 
outcomes \citep{brell2020labor, fasani2024refugees}. Although this evidence concerns evaluations or actual outcomes 
rather than beliefs about future integration, it provides reason to expect that host-country populations may also 
distinguish between otherwise comparable refugees and economic migrants when forming such expectations. This leads to 
the following hypothesis:\bigskip

H2: Holding all other migrant characteristics constant, respondents expect refugees to achieve less favorable 
labor-market integration outcomes than economic migrants.\bigskip

A related literature examines migrants' employability and labor-market experiences and shows that employment outcomes 
depend not only on migrants' individual characteristics, but also on organizational and institutional factors in the 
host country \citep{shirmohammadi2019understanding, farashah2023perceived}. Research on immigrant recruitment further 
demonstrates that employers’ perceptions can shape migrants’ employment opportunities and that migrant workers may face
discrimination and disadvantages in hiring and occupational outcomes \citep{almeida2015fitting, dietz2015skill}. 
However, this literature primarily examines migrants’ actual labor-market experiences or employers’ recruitment 
decisions rather than expectations held by the broader host-country population.

A smaller literature comes closer to examining such expectations from the perspective of the host-country population. 
\citet{niemann2024long}, for example, study how working adults in Germany form expectations about migrants' occupational 
future and integration efforts depending on migrant characteristics such as origin, skill level, and reason for migration. 
Related research demonstrates that individuals also hold beliefs about discrimination. 
\citet{haaland2023beliefs} document substantial heterogeneity in beliefs about racial discrimination in hiring in the United States.
Although their study concerns racial discrimination rather than migration, it demonstrates the conceptual distinction 
between beliefs about discrimination and actual discriminatory behavior. 

Taken together, existing research provides evidence on several individual dimensions of migrants’ expected integration, 
but evidence examining expectations across multiple economic and social outcomes remains limited. This thesis 
contributes to this literature by examining expectations concerning employment, occupational match, income, 
discrimination, and social integration for Ukrainian and Syrian refugee migrants and economic migrants in Germany.

\subsection{Differential Perceptions of Ukrainian and Syrian Migrants}

Earlier literature shows that responses to refugees in Germany already differed across groups 
and changed over time during and after the 2015–2016 refugee inflow. In particular, country of origin was associated 
with differences in acceptance of refugess, while other characteristics such as migration 
circumstances and family composition also shaped public responses \citep{bansak2016economic,
czymara2016deutschland, czymara2017refugees, plener2017unaccompanied, liebe2018welcome}. \citet{czymara2017refugees}, 
for example, show that following the events of New Year’s Eve 2015/2016, the acceptance of refugees generally increased, 
whereas the acceptance of immigrants of North African and Arab origin declined. Other research demonstrates that 
attitudes also varied with local exposure to the refugee inflow. \citet{albarosa2023forced}, for instance, 
document heterogeneous changes in attitudes across German regions depending on the size of the local refugee inflow.
Looking at a later period, \citet{przechomski2026level} find that, after an initial increase in negative sentiment, 
attitudes gradually became more positive between 2017 and 2021.

The arrival of Ukrainian refugees following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 provided a new context for examining 
differences in responses to refugee groups in Europe. Existing research demonstrates that Ukrainian refugees have generally received 
more favorable public and institutional responses than Syrian refugees \citep{moise2024european, drewski2025states, das2025changing}. 
Evidence from Germany similarly shows greater willingness to help Ukrainian refugees, more positive perceptions of their 
warmth and integration prospects, and greater solidarity toward them than toward Syrian refugees \citep{echterhoff2023gefluchtete,
schmidt2024hierarchien, stefaniak2025double}. These findings therefore suggest that public responses
are shaped not only by refugee status itself but also by refugees’ country of origin.

Several explanations have been proposed for these differences. Theories of group position and perceived group threat 
suggest that attitudes toward immigrant groups depend partly on how these groups are positioned and perceived relative 
to the host population. Building on the group-position perspective of \citet{blumer1958race}, \citet{quillian1995prejudice} 
argues that prejudice can reflect perceived collective threat and the relative position of the in-group and out-group. 
In the context of Ukrainian and Syrian migrants, existing studies similarly point to differences in perceived cultural 
similarity, threat, and deservingness \citep{echterhoff2023gefluchtete,
schmidt2024hierarchien, stefaniak2025double}. At the broader political level, \citet{drewski2025states} show that 
Syrian and Ukrainian refugees were framed differently in Germany and Poland and link these differences to cultural 
and geopolitical factors that shaped state responses to the two groups.

These findings provide reason to expect that these differences between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants may also be 
reflected in beliefs about their integration outcomes. In line with this literature, 
I develop the following hypothesis: \bigskip

H3a: Holding other migrant characteristics constant, respondents expect Ukrainian migrants to achieve more favorable 
labor-market and social integration outcomes than Syrian migrants. \bigskip

In addition, the previous discussion in Section 2.2 provides reason to expect that individuals also form beliefs 
about the discrimination in labor-market outcomes of migrants. Combined with the less favorable perceptions of Syrian 
relative to Ukrainian migrants documented in this section, I developed the following hypothesis: \bigskip

H3b: Holding other migrant characteristics constant, respondents expect Syrian migrants to face greater discrimination 
in the German labor market than Ukrainian migrants.\bigskip

Further, I am interested in whether the difference between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants may depend on their reason for 
migration. Much of the literature above discussing differential responses toward the two groups concerns refugees specifically, 
. Much of the literature above discussing differential responses toward the two groups concerns refugees specifically, 
suggesting that country of origin may be particularly consequential when migrants are evaluated in a refugee context. 
By contrast, evaluations of economic migrants may place relatively greater weight on characteristics related to their 
expected economic contribution. I therefore develop the following hypothesis: \bigskip

H3c: The difference in expected integration outcomes between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants is greater when migrants are 
presented as refugees than when they are presented as economic migrants.\bigskip

In addition, I explore whether differences between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants vary with their level of human capital, 
and in particular whether higher human capital reduces the country-of-origin gap in expected integration outcomes.

\subsection{Belief Formation Through Contact, Experience, Information, and Memory}

Intergroup contact is one potential channel through which individuals form and update perceptions of migrant groups. 
The contact hypothesis, originating with \citet{allport1954nature}, proposes that contact between members of different 
groups can reduce prejudice under optimal contact conditions. Among a large subsequent literature, as one major meta-analysis testing this hypothesis, 
\citet{pettigrew2006meta} find that intergroup contact is generally associated with lower 
prejudice but mainly that these effects are not limited to situations in which all of Allport's favorable conditions are satisfied.
Evidence from migration and refugee contexts similarly indicates that direct contact can be associated with more 
favorable evaluations of migrants and refugees \citep{kotzur2019meeting, de2021contact, khalil2022does}.

However, the amount of contact alone may not be sufficient to explain how intergroup experiences shape perceptions. 
Contact can generate different experiences, and its effects may therefore depend on the nature of the interactions that occur.
It is therefore important to explicitly distinguish between positive and negative intergroup contact.
\citet{laurence2018countervailing} show that greater ethnic diversity can generate opportunities for both positive 
and negative intergroup contact, where positive contact is associated with more favorable attitudes and perceptions of migrants and refugees, 
while negative contact is associated with less favorable attitudes and behavioral tendencies \citep{kotzur2019meeting, bagci2023dealing}.
Similarly, \citet{de2021contact} distinguish the frequency and valence of intergroup contact in the context of the 
European refugee crisis and find that the valence of direct contact is particularly important for attitudes toward 
refugees. Evidence from Germany also suggests that contact with foreigners can reduce immigration worries, while 
repeated contact over time does not necessarily strengthen this relationship \citep{khalil2022does}. Experimental 
and quasi-experimental evidence from other countries shows that increased exposure to refugees and individuals with a 
migration background can under some circumstances generate more exclusionary or hostile responses \citep{enos2019causal, hangartner2019does}.
These findings suggest that greater exposure to a migrant group should not automatically be interpreted as more 
favorable contact and motivate distinguishing the frequency of contact from the valence of the experiences it produces.

The importance of contact valence is consistent with a broader literature showing that personal social experiences 
can shape beliefs and behavior. \citet{schwerter2020determinants} experimentally show that positive and 
negative social experiences lead to substantial differences in subsequent willingness to trust, with beliefs about 
others' trustworthiness playing an important role in these effects. Applied to the migration context, this suggests that personal
experiences with members of a migrant group may be associated with subsequent beliefs and expectations about the group more broadly.
These findings provide a reason for considering not only whether respondents have interacted with Ukrainian and 
Syrian migrants, but also the negative versus positive experiences they have had with members of the respective migrant groups.

Individuals may also form evaluations based on the information about migration that they acquire beyond their own 
direct personal experiences. Experimental evidence shows that providing information 
about immigrants and refugees can affect subsequent perceptions and attitudes, although the direction and magnitude 
of these effects depend on the information provided and respondents' prior beliefs \citep{gunther2023would, 
hayo2023between, dylong2026intergroup}. \citet{dylong2026intergroup}, for example, find that providing accurate 
information about the size of the immigrant population in Eastern Germany leads to more supportive attitudes toward 
immigration, with stronger effects among respondents with less prior contact with immigrants. In contrast, 
\citet{guenther2024revisiting} finds that correcting respondents' underestimation of the number of asylum seekers 
increases opposition to immigration. Taken together, these findings suggest that external information represents an 
additional source from which individuals may form perceptions of migrant groups, and that its influence may be 
particularly relevant when direct personal experience is limited.

Media constitute an important source of information about migrant groups. The literature on mediated intergroup contact 
conceptualizes mass media as a form of indirect contact through which individuals can acquire information about 
out-groups even in the absence of direct personal interaction \citep{schiappa2005parasocial, de2021contact}. 
Importantly, however, the relationship between media exposure and attitudes depends not only on the amount of exposure 
but also on the content and framing of the information encountered. Media representations of immigrants and refugees 
frequently emphasize economic, cultural, or security-related threats \citep{atwell2016media}, while different 
types of media may portray refugees in systematically different ways \citep{jacobs2016news}. Previous research 
suggests that direct and mediated contact may interact, with positive direct contact potentially reducing individuals' 
sensitivity to negative media representations \citep{ahmed2017news}. Moreover, media exposure may itself be selective, 
as individuals can choose to consume refugee-related media content that is based partly on their existing views about refugees 
\citep{lueders2019refugees}. Taken together, this literature establishes media exposure as an important indirect source 
of information about migrant groups and suggests that both the content of this information and individuals' prior 
direct experiences may be relevant for how migrants are subsequently perceived and evaluated.

However, past experiences and information may not receive equal weight in memory when individuals form current beliefs.
The influence of past experiences and information on current beliefs may depend on what individuals recall when forming 
an evaluation. Models of associative memory suggest that recall is selective and cue-dependent, such that experiences 
related to the current context are more likely to come to mind and influence beliefs \citep{bordalo2023memory, enke2024associative}. 
Consistent with this mechanism, \citet{graeber2024stories} show that stories remain more influential on beliefs over 
time than statistical information because they are more likely to be recalled. Applied to the migration context, 
evaluations of Ukrainian and Syrian migrants may therefore depend not only on respondents’ overall exposure to each 
group, but also on which personal experiences, stories, and other information about the respective group come to mind 
when forming their beliefs.


Taken together, this literature suggests that beliefs about migrant groups may reflect not only the information individuals have encountered, but also how this information was acquired and which experiences are subsequently recalled. This may be particularly relevant for comparisons between Ukrainian and Syrian migrants in Germany, given differences in the timing and context of the two groups' large-scale arrival and, consequently, in respondents' opportunities for direct contact and exposure to group-related information. At the same time, greater exposure to a group does not necessarily imply more favorable experiences or beliefs. The present study therefore complements the experimental comparison of otherwise identical Ukrainian and Syrian migrant profiles with respondent-level measures of group-specific contact and experience, allowing these factors to be examined as correlates of differences in expected integration outcomes.
